The shop belongs to several people. Some put money in, some put work in, some both. Hanotna's capital ledger is their shared diary: what each partner put in, what the shop is worth, what a trading cycle earned, and how the profit is split. It is the feature the app exists for, and the one with the most rules.

### A ledger with no edits

Every injection, withdrawal and expense is a row in one ledger, in one of fifteen categories. A row is not edited once written, so every balance can be rebuilt from the ledger at any time. An expense entered by mistake is undone by a reverse entry that points at it, within the cycle it was made in. Partners can put money in as dinars, or in EUR, USD and stablecoins at a rate they type.

### Ownership follows the money

A partner's capital account is what they have really put in, and their share of the shop is that account over the total. Nobody owns a percentage by decree. A partner can also hold a work share: a fixed cut of each cycle's profit, taken before the investors are paid. The work shares together can never pass one hundred per cent, a rule the database enforces as well as the app.

### Trading cycles

Profit is measured over a cycle, and the owner closes it. Closing asks for a count of the cash, the stock and the debts, takes a photograph of the business, works out the cycle's profit, splits it, and opens the next cycle in the same transaction, so there is never a moment without one. A sale can be rung up only while a cycle is open.

When a cycle closes in profit, each partner's share can be paid out, put back into the shop as capital, or left for later.

### Counting the drawer

On the capital dashboard, the Cash collected row opens the drawer. A count records two numbers: what is taken to the safe and what is left in the drawer. The app compares their sum with what the drawer should hold, which is what was left at the last count plus the cash payments recorded since, and shows the answer at once: Match, Short or Over. The first count of a cycle sets the starting point. A drawer count does not change the ledger, so profit and the split are untouched by it. The count that corrects profit is the one the close asks for.

<PhoneFrame alt="The Cash collected screen." caption="Cash collected" />

Partners count. The owner alone can explain a difference or delete the latest count, and the drawer is not shown to staff. The payments behind the Cash collected figure are listed by day, and the list's total matches it to the dinar. Closing a cycle shows what went to the safe and the differences nobody explained, and it asks before closing if cash came in after the last count.

### Principal only, never interest

A partner can lend the shop money. The loan is tracked apart from ownership and repaid at face value. The app never models interest (riba) in any form, and the code has no field for an interest rate.

<ForEngineers>
  The headline profit is a flow calculation: revenue, minus the cost of the goods sold, minus expenses. The cost of goods comes from a snapshot taken at the moment of each sale. The count at the close reconciles that estimate: actual profit is the estimate plus the difference between each counted figure (cash, stock, customer debts, supplier debts) and its book figure. The growth in what the business is worth is the fallback for a cycle closed without that breakdown. Closing is one Firestore transaction that re-reads the cycle, writes the end snapshot and the split, opens the next cycle with that snapshot as its start, and flips a metadata flag that lets sales proceed. Only a system admin closes a cycle or writes a reverse entry. Money put in or taken out has no reverse entry, and a supplier bill's transport rows are deleted with the bill. The cap of one hundred per cent on work shares is enforced twice: an optimistic-concurrency aggregate in the app, and a rule on the database that reads the post-write state. Each drawer count stores what it was compared with, so history is not recomputed, and the database checks the chain: expected is what the previous count left plus the payments since. When two phones count at once, the second is refused.
</ForEngineers>
